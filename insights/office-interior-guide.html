Workplace

What workplace do you have?

Arrival, focus, and the demo room. An office interior guide built around how your people actually work.

28 Jul 2026 2 min read Woodex Studio · Lahore

Every office is three offices wearing one address: the one clients arrive into, the one people focus in, and the one where the company proves it can do the work. An office interior that serves only one of the three fails the other two daily.

Arrival — the room that speaks first

Reception is not a desk and a logo wall; it is the first claim the company makes. Visitors should read within seconds what the business is and how seriously it takes itself. The arrival sequence — door, threshold, reception, waiting — deserves drawing and 3D like any client-facing room.

Focus — the expensive silence

Open plans are cheap to build and expensive to work in. The design question is acoustic zoning: which teams need shelter, which need collision, and where the phone booths live. Materials decide this more than layout — a focused office is built from surfaces that absorb, not amplify.

The demo room — where deals close

Every firm has one: the room where the product, the portfolio or the pitch meets the client. It gets the best light, the best AV routing and the acoustic privacy the open plan cannot offer. Plan it first, not last — the demo room is the office's only revenue-generating room.

Gate factOffices run the same gates as residences — with one difference: the build usually has to happen around a working company. Sequencing is a design decision, made at Gate 2, not a contractor's afterthought.

What clients notice first

In order: light, sound, and the state of the meeting rooms. Not the brand wall. An office interior that photographs well but sounds like a canteen has failed its focus test. When we 3D an office, we render the workday scenario — screens on, blinds set, people implied — because that is the room the staff actually inherit.
